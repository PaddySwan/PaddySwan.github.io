Tracer

A daily maze speed game I designed and built from idea to launch. Six mazes, one clock.

Play today's Tracer

Overview

I love daily games, puzzles, and mazes. Tracer is my answer to "what if a daily game felt as good to play as the ones I keep coming back to?" Everyone gets the same six mazes each day, you race through them on one running clock, and every maze leaves a glowing trail behind you. The look is inspired by Flow Free's neon-on-dark line art.

It's also my first true game. I designed it, wrote the code, playtested it with friends, launched it, and have kept improving it since. It's live and free to play, with a new puzzle every day.

Before writing any game code, I wrote a one-page spec. It named the experience I wanted, fast, clean, skill-based, satisfying, and shareable, and it listed what V1 would not do: no enemies, no power-ups, no multiplayer, no leaderboard. A new player should understand the game within five seconds, so anything that didn't serve that got cut or pushed to a "later" list.

Then I shipped a small version and let it change from there. The spec grew from a plan into a changelog.

Design decisions

Movement that never gets in the way

Tracer is a speed game, so the controls have to keep up with you. The movement needed to feel smooth and satisfying, and it could never slow down a player who is racing. That took a lot of trial and error, with friends playtesting along the way.

Where I landed:

  • One tap moves exactly one cell, so tight turns stay precise.
  • Holding a key starts a fast repeat after a short delay, for long corridors.
  • Moves animate smoothly from cell to cell instead of snapping.
  • Arrow keys, WASD, or click and drag all work, including on a phone.

One shot a day, plus a place to warm up

In the spirit of a daily game, you get one shot: one run a day, and one final time to show for it. Early versions let you retry as often as you liked, so they tracked your best time across attempts. Once retries went away, that was no longer needed.

Still, as someone who plays a lot of daily games, I know the feeling of liking one and wanting more. So I added Practice: endless mazes, no clock pressure, so you can get a feel for the controls before you commit to today's run.

Six mazes, easy to hard

The spec said five mazes. I moved to six for a cleaner 3×2 recap grid, and because it gave players more to play: the mazes grow from 7×7 to 12×12, so the run ramps from easy to hard. It still lands at around a minute or two for an average player.

Mazes are generated from the date, so everyone gets the same puzzle. A quality check throws out layouts that are too short, so each one feels curated rather than random.

Turning a run into something you can share

Each maze gets its own trail color, so a finished run is a small piece of art. The recap shows all six trails, and the glyph overlays them into one abstract image that's different for every player and every day.

You can copy the glyph as a square image, or copy a text result with a colored dot for each maze. The goal was a result worth posting, not just a number.

Sound that tells you where you are

Sound is part of the base game. Every move makes a small tick, and its pitch climbs as you get closer to the goal, so you get a sense of your progress without looking. That came out of a conversation during development about whether someone who can't see the maze could still enjoy the game.

Blind Mode is an optional challenge built on that cue. The maze disappears and your marker is hidden, so all you have is sound: whether you're moving, and whether you're getting closer to or further from the end. It's a fun challenge, and it can be played by ear if you have a visual impairment.

Its sibling, Dark Mode, gives you a flashlight view of the maze with a randomized exit. Both live in Settings and are limited to Practice, so the daily run stays the same for everyone.

A few small extras

  • Sound effects for moves, cleared mazes, and finished runs. They're off by default, with a volume control.
  • Holiday themes that swap trail colors and wall colors on eight holidays.
  • A history calendar showing every day you've played, colored by speed.
  • A global reset at midnight UTC, with your local reset time shown on the landing screen.
  • A couple of easter eggs that started as jokes between friends. I'll leave those for you to find.

Build and process

Tracer is plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, drawn on a canvas with sound from the Web Audio API. There are no frameworks or dependencies, and the fonts are self-hosted, so it loads fast. Each system lives in its own file: maze generation, rendering, input, game state, and sound.

Every shipped change gets a revision number and a line in the changelog, the same way I'd track a product release.

A note on process: I wrote the code myself. When I got stuck, I used Claude Code to review logic and make quick, minor changes.

Reflection

Tracer taught me that feel is a design problem, not a polish step. The first versions worked on paper, but only playing them, and watching friends play them, showed where movement slowed people down. I also learned that constraints can be the feature: one run a day and a shared puzzle are what make the game a ritual.

Designing the movement sound cue was a reminder to ask early who a design leaves out, while it's still cheap to change.
