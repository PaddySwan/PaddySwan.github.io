Overview

PolyAI builds enterprise voice and chat agents that handle customer calls for companies across many industries, from restaurants to retail to healthcare. Picture a typical pharmacy phone menu: "press 1 for refills, press 2 for..." PolyAI's agents let you simply say what you need and get it handled, without a long wait for a person.

I worked mostly on healthcare projects, where calls are about scheduling, refills, and getting questions answered. Client details are confidential, so this page follows the story of my time there: the problems I worked on, how I approached them, and what changed.

What good looked like

Clients came to us to take pressure off their call centers, so some measures were simple. But a call can hit its numbers and still feel bad, so we cared about the experience too.

The numbers

  • Containment: the share of calls the agent handles start to finish without transferring to a human. If a client expects high containment and doesn't get it, the agent isn't doing its job.
  • Task success: for calls the agent should be able to handle, how many end with the job done, such as an appointment booked, rescheduled, or cancelled.

The experience

Callers should feel understood, not stuck in a loop or pushed away. Some clients put this above everything else. On one project, the client cared more about the overall experience than the success metrics. So I reviewed real calls not only for what failed, but for where a call felt awkward or frustrating.

How a call is designed

Every call follows a similar shape: greet the caller, understand what they need, respond, and wrap up. Most of the design work happens in the details, such as what happens when a caller is vague, changes their mind, or wants a person. Specific capabilities then add their own steps. Booking, for example, needs the agent to identify and verify the caller (IDNV) to find their account, and then negotiate a time.

What I worked on across projects

These challenges showed up in every project I touched, so I worked on them everywhere.

Earning the first few seconds

In a time of mistrust toward AI phone systems, the hardest problem was often the very start of a call. Some callers asked for a person right away, before the agent had said anything useful. I call this the first-turn "speak to" request, and it was the toughest handoff to reduce, since those callers never gave the agent a chance.

There was no single fix, so I tested several ideas over time and across projects:

  • A more convincing voice: if callers don't realize it's automated, fewer ask for a person.
  • A shorter introduction: trimming it from roughly 15-20 seconds to around 10 made it feel natural to respond instead of waiting. Getting to the caller's problem sooner helped, and shaving time anywhere we could made a difference.
  • Encouraging callers to speak freely: some callers had been trained by rigid phone menus not to, so a light nudge to state their request worked better than another menu.

The details that make a voice sound right

On voice projects, I worked with clients to choose the voice they wanted for their agent and tuned its settings to keep it stable. Then came the corrections that make an agent sound like a person instead of a machine. In healthcare these matter a lot: medication names, doctor names, office names, city names, and how numbers and addresses are read aloud.

Mispronouncing a drug name or reading a phone number as one long figure breaks trust, so I treated pronunciation as part of the design.

My time at PolyAI

My work grew over about a year and a half: from an existing project with a focused scope, to a larger and more complex one, to building new projects from scratch.

Learning the platform

After training on Agent Studio, PolyAI's platform for building agents (now called Dialog Studio), I took over an existing project with a focused scope. It was a good place to learn: FAQs, flows that give accurate information based on the caller's situation, and call reviews to improve the experience.

The FAQ work taught me the basics that I'd rely on for everything after. I tested whether the agent answered correctly, and when it didn't, I refined the prompt or the source information until the answer was what the client wanted. I also learned how to review a call and turn what I heard into a concrete improvement.

Growing with a key client

Next I took on an existing project for a long-standing client who was very important to the company. The agent had to keep growing as the client added offices and doctors, each with its own business rules, so I tracked those rules while updating the agent. Two problems stood out on this project.

Getting IDNV right

IDNV (identify and verify) is how the agent confirms who is calling, and finds their account, before it changes anything on it. It matters on every project where callers change something about their account. This specific struggle and these results are from this one project.

On this client's project, callers struggled to identify themselves because speech recognition kept mishearing their details. My engineer and I improved IDNV success from roughly 65% to over 90%, and it was still climbing at the end of my time there. The causes were mundane: "Sara" versus "Sarah," background noise, and numbers or IDs that mix letters and numbers, which got harder to capture the more complex they were. No single fix solved it, so we layered several:

  • Biasing: telling the system what answers to expect when we already knew them, instead of accepting anything it heard.
  • Less to collect: asking for less where we could, while still gathering enough for compliance.
  • Retry logic: changing how many attempts a caller got and rewording the retry so it was reassuring, which kept callers from bailing out to a human.
  • Readbacks: confirming details in a way that didn't disrupt the call, so we never looked up the wrong person.

Making slot negotiation loose and adaptive

The second problem was booking. Agreeing on a time is a negotiation: callers go back and forth between this week and next, mornings and afternoons, and change their minds. A rigid flow loses them, so I revised the booking flow to be loose and adaptive, while my engineer improved the booking system behind it.

Together these raised booking success by about 20% on this project, meaning more patients booked by phone with no human involved.

Building new agents from scratch

As my time at PolyAI went on, I started building new agents from the ground up. I built on what earlier projects and internal templates gave me, but shaped each agent's personality and behavior from scratch, taking it from requirements all the way to live callers. One example was an agent for a healthcare client rapidly expanding into new offices, to handle their initial calls. It booked and rescheduled appointments, took medication refill and transportation requests, and answered basic questions about locations.

It was among my most complex builds. There were many more business rules, and the flows needed more information collected and sent to the client's internal system. It also had to be bilingual: the agent used multilingual speech recognition that could pick up English or Spanish, and I prompted it on what to listen for and when to switch, even if the caller never asked.

Booking also needed more detail. Unlike my earlier project, where booking mostly used a general appointment type, the agent had to learn why someone was calling in order to book the right type. The hardest part was designing jump points into every step, because callers change their minds. It launched to live callers, and we kept improving it and adding features through the end of my time there.

Beyond the build

Launch was never the end. I reviewed calls to find where an agent could improve, fixed bugs, and helped our Deployment Strategists read dashboards and spot patterns, which decided where to focus next. Pods covered each other's projects, so I also had to be able to pick up another agent and keep it healthy.

I also built tools to make my teammates' days easier. Reviewing calls in other languages was a real struggle on multilingual projects, so I built a browser extension that adds translations right into the call review page. A second feature let you type a test message in English and have it translated for the agent in chat. It ran offline, so no sensitive call data was sent anywhere. Coworkers told me it made their daily work much smoother, and it was just one of a few extensions I built.

Tools & methods

Agent Studio Lucidchart Python VS Code Claude Code Wren Prompt design Call review Stakeholder interviews User acceptance testing Custom dashboards

I started in Agent Studio, mapped flows in Lucidchart, and wrote agent logic in Python in VS Code. Research was usually informal interviews, to understand who would be calling and what business rules the agent had to fit. Testing mixed manual calls from our team, pre-release testing with the client, and sometimes a trial period on one location so we could review real calls before expanding.

When AI coding tools arrived, I reviewed every change they made and tested the agent by hand afterward. Having written agents by hand first, I knew what the code should do. Later I used Wren, PolyAI's natural-language assistant, to build features alongside clients, falling back to manual code when needed.

Reflection

In about a year and a half, I went from maintaining an FAQ-focused agent, to growing a larger client's agent, to building new agents from scratch, including a bilingual healthcare agent. Conversation design suits how I think. I enjoy mapping the expected paths, where a caller could fall off the rails, and how to guide them back. It's fun and hard, because every caller arrives with their own idea of how the call should go. They have an understanding of what the agent should be able to do, and their own goals for the interaction. Designing for that means making room for many expectations at once, instead of just one.

The job also kept changing how I worked. I started in Agent Studio and wrote agent logic by hand in Python, then moved through AI coding tools like Cursor and Claude Code, and later Wren. Each one changed how quickly and carefully I could make changes. I tend to stick with a workflow once it's comfortable, so working through that discomfort was a real lesson. It paid off in my Python, which improved a lot, and in the browser tools I built for my teammates, which started with noticing a point of friction and being willing to build something new.

The feedback I valued most was from teammates who said they didn't have to worry about a project once I had it. It's what I want to be known for: reliable work that I understand from the technical side and the design side. The habit I'm still building is saying what I notice sooner. I often noticed things that were broken or could be better, both in projects and in our internal tooling. I'm learning to share what I notice and what I learn before it's needed, so the whole team benefits earlier.
